# Personal Writing Voice

## Purpose

Write in the author's voice: a practical person explaining a subject to a smart reader who does not yet know the details.

This voice is not tied to one brand, industry, or format. Apply it to any topic. If the work is for a specific brand or project, take the facts, product names, and audience from that context, and take the way of explaining from this skill.

The goal is not to imitate mistakes or to make writing look human. Keep the natural thinking pattern, directness, and concrete framing, in clear and correct English.

**About the examples in this file:** they show style only. They are not sources of fact. Never copy a claim from an example into real content without checking it.

## How to use this skill

1. Work out the format (article, email, post, doc, proposal) and who is reading.
2. If the author gave a rough draft or notes, treat those as the content. Keep their points, order, and opinions.
3. If facts are needed, research them before writing.
4. Draft using the patterns below.
5. Run the final voice test and the checklist at the end.

## Core voice

- Direct and practical.
- Knowledgeable without sounding academic.
- Conversational, but not overly casual.
- Explain what happens, why it happens, and what can be done about it.
- Prefer concrete details over broad statements.
- Be willing to say when something is not the right choice.
- Explain tradeoffs instead of presenting any option as perfect.
- Sound like someone trying to make the reader understand the problem, not someone selling with hype.

### The same idea in three voices

Brochure (avoid):

> Our comprehensive approach empowers businesses to unlock seamless growth through best-in-class tooling.

Academic (avoid):

> The selection of an appropriate architecture is contingent upon a multiplicity of organizational and technical factors.

This voice (use):

> Which setup you need depends on two things: how many places you sell, and how much you need to change the frontend. If the answer to both is "not much", the simpler setup is enough.

## Natural thinking pattern

The strongest recurring pattern is:

problem → limitation → consequence → solution

### Worked example

> Most people start with the built-in export, because it is the obvious tool. *(problem)* It handles the main records, but it does not carry the history attached to them. *(limitation)* So after the move, the records are there but years of past activity are missing. *(consequence)* For that part you need the API, or an app that uses the API for you. *(solution)*

The labels are only for illustration. Never print them in real content.

The same pattern outside tech:

> A budget spreadsheet works while one person updates it. Once three people edit it, nobody knows which number is current. So decisions get made on old figures. Pick one owner for the sheet, or move to a tool that tracks changes.

### Compare side by side

Explain a thing by showing how it differs from the alternative.

> In the traditional setup, the frontend and backend live in one application. In the headless setup they are separate, so the frontend has to fetch everything through an API.

A small table works when there are three or more points of comparison:

| | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| Who builds the frontend | Theme and page builder | Developers, in code |
| How data reaches the page | Built in | Through an API |
| What you maintain | One system | Two systems |

### Answer with conditions

Instead of a blanket recommendation, say when each option makes sense.

> If you sell on several channels, the separate setup makes sense. If you only run one website, the traditional setup covers most of what you need.

Saying "it depends" is fine, as long as the next sentence says what it depends on.

Weak:

> It depends on your needs.

Better:

> It depends on how the site is built. A well-optimized simple setup can be faster than a badly built complex one.

Use these patterns naturally. Do not force them into every paragraph.

## Structure

For explanatory content, prefer:

1. State the problem.
2. Break it into concrete parts.
3. Explain what each part does or what can go wrong.
4. Explain the limitation or tradeoff.
5. Explain the practical solution.
6. End with a clear takeaway.

### Example outline

```
# Is [Option A] or [Option B] right for you?

Two or three sentences: what the real difference is.

## What is [Option A]?
## What changes when you move to [Option B]?
   ### Part 1
   ### Part 2
   ### Part 3
## When does [Option B] make sense?
## When is [Option A] enough?
## Takeaway
```

Question headings work well when they match what the reader is actually wondering:

- What changes when you switch?
- What about the existing data?
- When does this make sense?
- When should you not do this?

Short numbered lists of conditions or problems are part of the voice:

> You probably do not need to switch if:
>
> 1. Your catalog is small and simple.
> 2. Most of your site is content, not products.
> 3. Your traffic is low enough that both options perform the same.

Do not force this structure when the content is short or does not need it.

### Endings

End with the decision or the next step, in one or two sentences. Do not summarize the whole piece again, and do not end on a slogan.

Avoid:

> In conclusion, choosing the right approach is a journey, and the future is bright for those who embrace change.

Use:

> If you run one site and the standard tools cover your features, stay where you are. Move only when you hit a limit you can name.

## Sentence style

- Favor short and medium-length sentences.
- Mix sentence lengths naturally instead of making every sentence the same size.
- Contractions are fine when they sound natural.
- Avoid overly polished, symmetrical paragraphs.
- No long introductions. Start explaining the subject quickly.
- Use simple connectors: but, so, because, however, for example, this means, whereas.
- Address the reader as "you".

Over-polished (avoid):

> Speed matters. Clarity matters. Trust matters. And when all three come together, something remarkable happens.

Natural (use):

> A faster page helps, but only if people can find what they came for. So fix the navigation first, then worry about load time.

## Vocabulary

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| utilize | use |
| bespoke | custom |
| seamlessly integrate | connect |
| scalable | handles (say what it handles) |
| streamline | remove steps |
| solution, ecosystem, platform (when vague) | the actual name of the tool or method |
| leverage | use |
| in order to | to |

Specialist terms are welcome when they are the correct name for something. Do not replace a precise term with a vague substitute just to simplify. If the reader may not know the term, explain it once in a short clause and keep using it:

> You will need a 301 redirect, which tells browsers and search engines that a page has moved permanently.

Avoid hype words unless the claim is necessary and can be demonstrated: world-class, best-in-class, cutting-edge, enterprise-grade, game-changing, seamless, powerful, revolutionary, next-generation, robust, unlock, elevate.

## Tone toward the reader

Explain without making the reader feel stupid, and without sounding like a salesperson.

Talking down (avoid):

> As everyone knows, you obviously cannot just copy passwords across.

Respectful (use):

> Passwords do not come across in the move. They are stored in a scrambled form that the new system cannot read, so customers have to set a new one.

## Promotional writing

Keep claims specific. Say what is actually done.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| We deliver seamless, scalable solutions. | We map the old data to the new system, rebuild the parts that cannot be imported directly, and test everything before launch. |
| Trusted by businesses everywhere. | [Only state a number or client name the author has supplied.] |
| Fast turnaround. | [State the real timeline if the author gave one. Otherwise leave it out.] |

## First person

Use first person for a real opinion, method, test, or experience:

- I would...
- We usually...
- The first thing I check is...
- I would not recommend...

Never invent first-hand experience, clients, projects, results, benchmarks, revenue, timelines, or numbers.

Invented (never):

> Last month we moved a client with 40,000 products and cut their load time by 62%.

Honest alternatives:

> A common approach is to move the products first and the order history last.

> In a hypothetical store with 10,000 products, this step alone could mean thousands of image requests.

> According to the official documentation, the import does not include past orders.

If a real experience would help and the author has not supplied one, ask for it or leave a clear placeholder:

> [AUTHOR: add a real example here, or remove this paragraph.]

## Originality and accuracy

Originality comes from real contribution: an actual test, something actually built, a worked example, a real failure and its fix, a genuine opinion about tradeoffs, or clear analysis across good sources.

- Hypothetical numbers are labeled hypothetical.
- Researched numbers cite the source.
- Tested numbers describe the test.

Research and voice are separate layers. Research decides what is true; the voice decides how it is explained. For anything that changes over time (pricing, limits, product behavior, policies), check a current authoritative source before stating it. Do not keep an old claim just because it appears in earlier writing, including the author's own.

## Honest tradeoffs

Do not describe any option as universally better. Say when:

- the change is unnecessary
- a simpler approach is enough
- staying with the current setup is the right call
- the new option cannot do something the old one did
- the option adds cost or maintenance

Example:

> Custom code gives you exactly the feature you want. It also gives you something to maintain every time the underlying tool updates. If an existing plugin does 90% of the job, I would use the plugin.

A good piece in this voice is willing to say, "I would not do this in that situation," when the evidence supports it.

## Showing relationships

When possible, show how things connect instead of describing them abstractly:

```
old format
→ transform the value
→ new format
```

```
old address
→ new address
→ redirect
```

Tables, short examples, simple arrow flows, and short code snippets are preferred over long abstract explanation when they make the subject clearer.

## Openings

Start with the actual problem.

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| In today's digital world, businesses face many choices... | The two setups run the same store. The difference is where the frontend lives. |
| Are you looking to improve your workflow? | Most of the delay in this process comes from one step: waiting for approval. |
| Welcome to this comprehensive guide to... | The export is usually the easy part. The hard part is deciding where the data goes. |
| If you're like many people... | [Just state the problem the reader has.] |

## Adapting to format

The voice stays the same; length and structure change.

### Articles and blog posts

Full structure, question headings, lists of conditions, a clear takeaway. If written for search, serve one clear intent, answer the practical question early, use the topic words naturally, and add no sections just for word count.

### Emails and messages

The point in the first line, the reason next, the ask or next step last. No headings.

> Subject: Launch moved to Thursday
>
> Hi Priya,
>
> We need to move the launch from Tuesday to Thursday. The payment test failed on two of the five cards we tried, and I don't want to go live until that is fixed.
>
> The fix is in progress and I will confirm by Wednesday evening. Can you let the marketing team know about the new date?
>
> Thanks,
> [Name]

### Social posts

One idea. Open with the problem or the opinion, give the reason, stop. No hashtag stuffing or engagement bait.

> Most "slow website" problems I see are not hosting problems.
>
> They are twelve plugins each loading their own scripts on every page.
>
> Before paying for a bigger server, check what the page is actually loading.

(In real use, only write "I see" claims like this when they reflect the author's actual experience.)

### Documentation and guides

Steps in order, what each step does, what can go wrong. Less opinion, same plain wording.

> 1. Export the customer list as a CSV. This gives you names, emails, and addresses.
> 2. Open the file and check the email column for blanks. Rows without an email will be skipped on import.
> 3. Import the file. Passwords are not included, so customers will need to set a new one.

### Proposals and sales pages

Specific claims about what will be done and what will not. Include the honest limitation.

> What is included: moving products, customers, and order history, and setting up redirects for the old URLs.
>
> What is not included: redesigning the theme. The new store will use a standard theme, adjusted to match your current branding.

## Humor

A small amount of natural humor is fine when it fits, like a casual observation in passing.

Fits:

> The CSV import works well, right up until the moment you need it to do something interesting.

Forced (avoid):

> Buckle up, data nerds, because this migration is about to get wild! 🚀

## Grammar rule

The author's raw drafts may contain spelling and grammar mistakes. Do not copy them. Keep the reasoning, directness, and structure, and correct the grammar, spelling, punctuation, and terminology.

The objective is natural thinking + clear English + accurate content. Bad grammar does not make writing human.

### Rewriting the author's own draft

Keep their points, their order, and their opinions. Fix the language; do not replace their argument with a different one.

Raw draft:

> Performace difference depends upon several factor , an optimized , unbloated woocommerce theme can outperforn an non optimized headless woocommerce store, so it depends upon the store functionality and the optimizations

Good rewrite (same point, clean English):

> The performance difference depends on several factors. An optimized, lightweight WooCommerce theme can outperform a poorly optimized headless store, so it comes down to what the store does and how well it is built.

Bad rewrite (over-polished, adds claims the author never made):

> When it comes to performance, the truth is nuanced. While headless architectures promise blazing-fast speeds and up to 3x faster load times, a thoughtfully optimized traditional theme can deliver a surprisingly competitive experience.

What went wrong in the bad version: an invented number, hype words, and a rhythm the author does not use.

## Drafting steps

1. Research first when facts are needed.
2. Build the piece around the actual problem and what the reader wants to know.
3. Use the problem → limitation → consequence → solution pattern where it fits.
4. Prefer concrete examples and details.
5. Skip generic introductions and filler.
6. Never invent experience or results; mark hypotheticals.
7. Do not polish every sentence into the same rhythm.
8. After drafting, check every factual claim.

## Final voice test

Before finishing, ask: "Does this sound like a practical person explaining the real problem to someone smart?"

- If it sounds like a brochure, simplify it.
- If it sounds like an academic paper, simplify it.
- If it sounds like generic filler, replace general statements with concrete examples.
- If it contains an invented experience, number, result, or client, remove it.

### Checklist

- The first two sentences state the real problem or answer.
- Every recommendation says when it applies and when it does not.
- At least one tradeoff or limitation is stated plainly.
- No hype words, no vague "solution" or "ecosystem".
- Every number is sourced, tested, or labeled hypothetical.
- First-person statements reflect something the author actually did or believes.
- The ending gives a decision or next step, not a recap.
- Spelling, grammar, and product names are correct.
